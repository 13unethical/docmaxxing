Turnitin flags essays that a person wrote. It happens often enough that a high AI score, on its own, is a weak accusation. Detectors score statistical patterns — even rhythm, stock phrases, very regular grammar — and careful student writing produces those patterns without any chatbot in the room.

If a teacher has already asked you about a score, the useful response is evidence of how you wrote, plus a revision of the sentences that actually look AI-like. Panic-rewriting the whole essay overnight reads worse than a calm explanation.

## Why human writing gets flagged

The model has never seen "you." It has seen millions of chatbot answers, and it flags whatever sits close to them. That includes:

- **Template essays.** The five-paragraph shape, "In today's society," a definition, three reasons, "In conclusion."
- **Heavy grammar tools.** Grammarly and similar editors smooth wording into the even style detectors associate with AI. A full QuillBot pass does this even more. [Does Turnitin detect ChatGPT, Grammarly and QuillBot?](/blog/does-turnitin-detect-chatgpt)
- **Non-native writing that was edited hard.** Simpler vocabulary and careful, regular sentences raise scores on several detectors. The writing is yours; the rhythm is what the model noticed.
- **Short submissions.** A few unusual sentences swing a 200-word answer much more than they swing a 2,000-word paper.
- **Text you did draft with AI and then rewrote lightly.** A synonym pass leaves the sentence shape in place, and current reports can mark that as AI-paraphrased.

The mechanics are in [how AI detectors work](/blog/how-ai-detectors-work).

## What to take to the conversation

Instructors are told not to decide from the score alone. Give them something else to look at:

1. **Version history.** Google Docs and Word keep it. A file that grew over a week, with your deletions and the messy middle drafts, is the strongest thing you can show. A file created an hour before the deadline, in one paste, is the opposite.
2. **Notes, outlines, annotated readings.** Photos of handwritten notes count.
3. **An explanation of the argument** in the room, without the document in front of you. Being able to walk through your sources is more persuasive than disputing the percentage.
4. **An honest account of tools.** If you used a grammar checker, a translator or AI to brainstorm, say so and check it against the course rules. Hiding a grammar tool and then having it come out later is a worse position.

A **\*%** result is a small amount of AI-like text, not a hidden high score. If that is what they are asking about, start with [what the asterisk means](/blog/turnitin-ai-score-asterisk).

## If they want a resubmission

Ask which parts concerned them, or find those parts yourself. Paste the essay into the [free AI detector](/ai-detector) and rewrite only the highlighted sentences: different lengths, a concrete detail from your notes, your own verbs. Keep the citations and the numbers.

Rewriting everything in a panic just to "sound more human" often produces another even, generic draft and a similar score. If a highlighted section is long, the [AI humanizer](/humanizer) can redo the wording; read it and keep only the sentences that still say what you meant. Then run the detector again.

## Key takeaways

- A flag is a pattern match, not proof. Formulaic, heavily edited and non-native writing gets caught.
- Bring version history, notes and a clear account of the tools you used.
- Revise the highlighted sentences, not the whole essay in one panicked pass.
