Yes. Turnitin's AI writing indicator is built to catch prose that reads like ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and the other chatbots students actually use — and, in current reports, prose that was generated and then paraphrased. Grammarly and QuillBot don't get a free pass either: heavy rewriting by those tools often smooths an essay into the same even style the detector is looking for.

It is still an estimate, not proof. Here is what it tends to catch, what slips past, and how to see your own result before a teacher does.

## What Turnitin is actually detecting

The AI report scores **sentences**, then rolls that up into a percentage of your qualifying prose. Qualifying prose means the long-form sentences. Headings, reference lists, tables and bullet points usually don't count, which is why a document can "feel" long and still get no score: there wasn't enough prose to judge.

The patterns it reacts to are the ones chatbots default to:

- sentences of a similar length and shape, one after another
- stock openers and transitions ("In today's world," "It is important to note," "Furthermore")
- claims with no specific detail from your course, your reading or your data
- a perfectly even tone from the first paragraph to the last

A basic paraphraser changes the vocabulary and leaves those patterns in place. That's why "I ran it through QuillBot" is not a reliable way past the check, and why Turnitin added a category for AI text that has been paraphrased.

## What it misses or gets wrong

- **Short answers.** Below a minimum amount of prose, Turnitin often shows no AI score at all. A 150-word discussion post is a different case from a 2,000-word essay.
- **Genuinely mixed writing.** A paragraph of your own notes followed by a paragraph of chatbot prose is scored sentence by sentence, so the percentage can land anywhere.
- **Human writing that looks like a template.** Formulaic essays, heavy grammar-tool editing, and some non-native writing get flagged even when nobody used a chatbot. We go through why in [How AI detectors work](/blog/how-ai-detectors-work).
- **Low scores.** When the estimate is small, the report shows **\*%** instead of a number, because the exact figure wouldn't be reliable. See [what \*% means](/blog/turnitin-ai-score-asterisk).

So "Turnitin didn't flag it" does not prove a text was written by a person, and a high score does not prove it wasn't.

## How to check before you submit

You can't see Turnitin's AI report as a student in most courses — instructors can. The practical version is to preview the draft yourself:

1. Paste it into the [free AI detector](/ai-detector). It highlights the sentences that look AI-written, which is the part worth your time. It's our own model, tuned on real Turnitin reports, so treat it as a preview rather than the official number.
2. Rewrite the highlighted sentences yourself, or run a long flagged passage through the [AI humanizer](/humanizer), then read the output and fix anything that drifted from your point.
3. Run the detector again. Repeat on whatever is still highlighted.

If your course allows the AI help you used, say so the way the course asks (APA 7 has a format for citing ChatGPT; we cover it in [Как процитировать ChatGPT по APA 7](/ru/blog/kak-procitirovat-chatgpt-apa-7)). If it doesn't, rewriting the flagged lines is the part of the job that was always yours.

## Key takeaways

- Turnitin's AI report does flag ChatGPT-style prose, including text that was only paraphrased.
- Grammarly and QuillBot can push writing toward the same patterns when they rewrite heavily.
- The score is an estimate of qualifying prose, and it is wrong in both directions often enough that nobody should treat it as proof.
- Preview the draft with the [free AI detector](/ai-detector), rewrite what it highlights, and check again.
